# Chapter 4 The golden word and returns

[Chapter 1](ch01-golden-deficit.md) added the Zeckendorf digits of two natural numbers and studied the deficit left behind by the carries. This chapter takes another view: listing the lowest Zeckendorf digit of every natural number in turn gives an infinite word over two letters. It is the golden word generated by a substitution rule ([@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf]), and it has exactly $n+1$ distinct blocks of each length $n$ ([@thm-factor-complexity]). The last two sections turn to the returns of the golden rotation and to one use of the deficit on prime factorizations.

The chapter uses the following terms.

Word
: A finite or infinite sequence of letters. The length of a finite word $u$ is $|u|$; letters are counted from position $0$.

Factor
: A finite word made of consecutive letters of an infinite word, starting at some position. The only factor of length $0$ is the empty word.

$\mathrm{zeck}(i)$
: The Fibonacci indices used by the Zeckendorf representation of the natural number $i$, in decreasing order: `wdigits i` in Lean (mathlib's `Nat.zeckendorf i`). Indices follow mathlib's convention $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = F_2 = 1$, $F_3 = 2$: every index is at least $2$, and no two are adjacent. For example $12 = F_6 + F_4 + F_2$, so $\mathrm{zeck}(12) = [6, 4, 2]$. For existence and uniqueness see [@thm-zeckendorf-exists] and [@thm-zeckendorf-unique] in [Appendix A](appendix-a-zeckendorf.md).

::: {.definition #def-golden-word title="The golden word" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenWord.goldenWord"}
Take two letters $\mathtt{a}$ and $\mathtt{b}$, standing for `true` and `false` in Lean. The substitution $\sigma$ is

$$
\sigma(\mathtt{a}) = \mathtt{ab}, \qquad \sigma(\mathtt{b}) = \mathtt{a},
$$

applied to a finite word letter by letter, with the results concatenated in order. Let

$$
w_0 = \mathtt{a}, \qquad w_{Q+1} = \sigma(w_Q) \quad (Q \in \mathbb{N}).
$$

The golden word is the infinite word $g = g(0)\,g(1)\,g(2) \cdots$ in which $g(i)$ is letter $i$ of $w_i$.
:::

The definition reads along the diagonal: letter $i$ is taken from level $i$. This always works, because $|w_Q| = F_{Q+2}$ (`fibWord_length` in trureturing) and $i < F_{i+2}$. The first levels are:

| $Q$ | $w_Q$ | length $F_{Q+2}$ |
| ---: | :--- | ---: |
| 0 | $\mathtt{a}$ | 1 |
| 1 | $\mathtt{ab}$ | 2 |
| 2 | $\mathtt{aba}$ | 3 |
| 3 | $\mathtt{abaab}$ | 5 |
| 4 | $\mathtt{abaababa}$ | 8 |
| 5 | $\mathtt{abaababaabaab}$ | 13 |
| 6 | $\mathtt{abaababaabaababaababa}$ | 21 |

Each level is a prefix of the next: $w_0 = \mathtt{a}$ is a prefix of $w_1 = \mathtt{ab}$, and if $u$ is a prefix of $u'$ then $\sigma(u)$ is a prefix of $\sigma(u')$. So all levels are prefixes of one infinite word, and $g(i)$ can be read from any level longer than $i$ (`goldenWord_eq_fibWord_get` in trureturing); in this sense $g$ is the limit of the $w_Q$. For instance, the first 21 letters of $g$ are $w_6$. The Lean definition takes the diagonal directly and needs no notion of limit.

::: {.proposition #prop-golden-word-zeckendorf title="The golden word and the lowest Zeckendorf digit" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenWord.goldenWord_char_zeckendorf"}
For every natural number $i$,

$$
g(i) = \mathtt{a} \iff 2 \notin \mathrm{zeck}(i).
$$

In words: $g(i) = \mathtt{a}$ exactly when the Zeckendorf representation of $i$ does not use the weight $F_2 = 1$.
:::

The table lists $0 \le i \le 20$. The letters $g(i)$ were computed from the substitution, and $\mathrm{zeck}(i)$ by the greedy algorithm (repeatedly take the largest Fibonacci number not exceeding what is left). Both agree with the proposition for all $i < 3000$; this is a finite check, not a proof.

| $i$ | $\mathrm{zeck}(i)$ | $g(i)$ | $i$ | $\mathrm{zeck}(i)$ | $g(i)$ |
| ---: | :--- | :---: | ---: | :--- | :---: |
| 0 | $[\,]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 11 | $[6, 4]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 1 | $[2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 12 | $[6, 4, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ |
| 2 | $[3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 13 | $[7]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 3 | $[4]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 14 | $[7, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ |
| 4 | $[4, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 15 | $[7, 3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 5 | $[5]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 16 | $[7, 4]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 6 | $[5, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 17 | $[7, 4, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ |
| 7 | $[5, 3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 18 | $[7, 5]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 8 | $[6]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | 19 | $[7, 5, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ |
| 9 | $[6, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 20 | $[7, 5, 3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |
| 10 | $[6, 3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ | | | |

::: proof
The Lean proof does not compare position by position; it shows that both sides obey the same recursion. On one side, induction on $Q$ gives $w_{Q+2} = w_{Q+1}\,w_Q$. On the other, for $0 \le j < F_{Q+2}$ the list $\mathrm{zeck}(F_{Q+3} + j)$ is $\mathrm{zeck}(j)$ with the index $Q+3$ put in front (`wdigits_fib_add` in trureturing); since $Q + 3 \ne 2$, positions $F_{Q+3} + j$ and $j$ get the same verdict. So the word of length $F_{Q+2}$ written down by the criterion $2 \notin \mathrm{zeck}(i)$ obeys the same recursion and also starts with $\mathtt{a}$ and $\mathtt{ab}$, hence equals $w_Q$ at every level. Reading letter $i$ at level $i$ gives the proposition. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

[@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf] describes single letters; the next theorem is about consecutive letters. The factor of length $n$ at position $i$ is $g(i)\,g(i+1) \cdots g(i+n-1)$.

::: {.theorem #thm-factor-complexity title="Exactly $n+1$ factors of length $n$" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenFactorComplexity.golden_factor_complexity"}
For every natural number $n$, the golden word has exactly $n + 1$ distinct factors of length $n$:

$$
\#\bigl\{\, g(i)\,g(i+1) \cdots g(i+n-1) \;:\; i \in \mathbb{N} \,\bigr\} = n + 1 .
$$
:::

For $n = 0$ the set contains only the empty word. The table lists the factors for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$, with the position of the first occurrence in parentheses. They were found in the prefix $w_{25}$, of length $F_{27} = 196418$. Such a computation only shows that these factors occur; it cannot show that no other factor appears further out, which is what the theorem says.

| $n$ | factors of length $n$ (first occurrence) | count |
| ---: | :--- | ---: |
| 1 | $\mathtt{a}$ (0), $\mathtt{b}$ (1) | 2 |
| 2 | $\mathtt{aa}$ (2), $\mathtt{ab}$ (0), $\mathtt{ba}$ (1) | 3 |
| 3 | $\mathtt{aab}$ (2), $\mathtt{aba}$ (0), $\mathtt{baa}$ (1), $\mathtt{bab}$ (4) | 4 |
| 4 | $\mathtt{aaba}$ (2), $\mathtt{abaa}$ (0), $\mathtt{abab}$ (3), $\mathtt{baab}$ (1), $\mathtt{baba}$ (4) | 5 |

For example, $\mathtt{bb}$ never occurs: every level $w_{Q+1} = \sigma(w_Q)$ is a concatenation of the blocks $\mathtt{ab}$ and $\mathtt{a}$, every $\mathtt{b}$ ends a block $\mathtt{ab}$, and the next block always begins with $\mathtt{a}$.

$n + 1$ is very few: an infinite word that has at most $n$ factors of length $n$ for some $n$ is periodic from some position on[^sturm]. Among words that are not eventually periodic, the complexity in [@thm-factor-complexity] is therefore the smallest possible; words whose factor complexity is exactly $n+1$ are called Sturmian words.

::: proof
The Lean proof follows the classical route for Sturmian words. It first writes the golden word as a difference of floors: the number of $\mathtt{a}$'s among the $m$ letters starting at position $i$ is

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{i+m+1}{\varphi} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{i+1}{\varphi} \right\rfloor
$$

(`goldenWindowTrueCount_eq_floor` in trureturing, derived from [@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf] through the Beatty sequence). Write $\{x\}$ for the fractional part and $x_i = \{(i+1)/\varphi\}$ for the phase; since $1/\varphi = \varphi - 1$, this is the $p(i)$ of [@thm-golden-phase]. For $1 \le m \le n$, the count above is $\lfloor m/\varphi \rfloor$, plus $1$ when $x_i \ge 1 - \{m/\varphi\}$. These $n$ cut points $1 - \{m/\varphi\}$ are distinct (because $1/\varphi$ is irrational) and divide $[0, 1)$ into $n + 1$ pieces. The factor of length $n$ is recovered from the counts by taking successive differences, so it depends only on the piece that contains $x_i$; hence there are at most $n + 1$ factors. Conversely, the phases $x_i$ are dense in $[0, 1)$, so every piece contains one, and phases in different pieces give different factors; hence there are at least $n + 1$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

::: {.figure src="factor-skeleton"}
**The skeleton of the proof.** The dashed box is the starting point, the neutral boxes are the steps of the proof (in grey monospace, the trureturing lemma that does the step), and the two branches give the upper and the lower bound, meeting in the green [@thm-factor-complexity].
:::

[^sturm]: M. Morse, G. A. Hedlund, Symbolic dynamics, *Amer. J. Math.* 60 (1938), 815–866; for Sturmian words see their Symbolic dynamics II. Sturmian trajectories, *Amer. J. Math.* 62 (1940), 1–42. This is a result from the literature, not formalized in this book: the cited Lean declaration only counts the factors of the golden word and does not contain this periodicity theorem. The other direction is elementary: an eventually periodic word has only boundedly many distinct factors, while $n + 1$ is unbounded, so the golden word is not eventually periodic.

## Returns {#sec-returns}

In the proof of [@thm-factor-complexity], the golden word is governed by the phase $x_i$, and $x_{i+1}$ is $x_i$ turned by $1/\varphi$ around a circle of circumference $1$. Since $\varphi = 1 + 1/\varphi$, turning by $1/\varphi$ and turning by $\varphi$ are the same rotation. When the phase comes back into the same piece, the same factor reappears; how soon factors recur depends on how soon this rotation comes back close to where it started.

::: {.figure src="golden-rotation"}
**The golden word codes the golden rotation.** The phase circle is drawn as the equator of a ball, and each step turns it about the vertical axis by $1/\varphi$ (the arrow from $x_0$ to $x_1$). With $n = 1$ the cut point $1 - \{1/\varphi\} = 1/\varphi^2$ splits the circle in two: a phase in the cyan piece gives $g(i) = \mathtt{a}$, a phase in the magenta piece $g(i) = \mathtt{b}$; the dots $x_0, \dots, x_7$ read $\mathtt{abaababa}$ in turn.
:::

A finite cyclic window is restored exactly after one full loop (trureturing calls this a revival; see the `CyclicWindowRevival` module). The golden rotation is never restored exactly: after $q$ steps its distance from the start is the distance $\norm{q\varphi}$ from $q\varphi$ to the nearest integer, which is not $0$ for any $q \ge 1$. Along Fibonacci times, [`fibonacci_golden_residual`](lean:D5/S1/Scale/FibonacciErrorRatio.fibonacci_golden_residual) gives the exact residual

$$
F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} = -\left(-\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)^{n},
$$

so for $n \ge 2$ the integer nearest to $F_n\varphi$ is $F_{n+1}$, and $\norm{F_n\varphi} = \varphi^{-n}$. The error shrinks like $\varphi^{-n}$ while the waiting time $F_n$ grows on the order of $\varphi^{n}$; their product $F_n\,|F_n\varphi - F_{n+1}|$ is called the revival score.

::: {.theorem #thm-revival-score title="The limit of the revival score" lean="D5/S3/ObserverMemory/GoldenRevivalScore.golden_fibonacci_revival_score_tendsto"}
Let $F_n$ be the Fibonacci numbers ($F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$) and $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$. Then

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} F_n \left| F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} \right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}.
$$
:::

::: proof
By the residual formula above, $|F_n\varphi - F_{n+1}| = \varphi^{-n}$. Binet's formula $F_n = (\varphi^n - \psi^n)/\sqrt5$ (`Real.coe_fib_eq` in mathlib), with $\psi = -1/\varphi$, then gives the exact closed form

$$
F_n \left| F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} \right| = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}\,\varphi^{-n} = \frac{1 - (-\psi^2)^n}{\sqrt5}.
$$

Since $\psi^2 = 1/\varphi^2 < 1$, the correction $(-\psi^2)^n$ tends to $0$. The Lean proof establishes exactly this closed form and then passes to the limit. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

Each score was computed both from the definition and from the closed form (60 significant digits); this is a computation, not a proof.

## A use for the deficit {#sec-almost-additive}

[@thm-deficit-three-valued] says that the deficit takes only the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$. This section applies that bound to the exponents of a prime factorization. For a prime $p$ and a positive integer $n$, write $v_p(n)$ for the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $n$.

$\lambda_-(n)$
: For a positive integer $n$, $\lambda_-(n) = \sum_{p \mid n} \beta'(v_p(n)) \log p$, summed over the prime factors of $n$, where $\beta'$ is the contraction-face reading of Chapter 1 ([@def-beta-contraction]) and $\log$ is the natural logarithm. This is `lambdaMinus` in Lean; it is also defined for $n = 0$ (with value $0$), which we do not need.

$\operatorname{rad}(k)$
: The product of the distinct prime factors of a positive integer $k$, with $\operatorname{rad}(1) = 1$. This is `primeRadical` in Lean.

Replacing $\beta'$ by $v \mapsto v$ would turn the sum into $\sum_{p \mid n} v_p(n) \log p = \log n$, which is exactly additive under multiplication. $\beta'$ is not additive, and neither is $\lambda_-$: $\lambda_-(2) = \beta'(1) \log 2 = \psi^2 \log 2 \approx 0.2648$ and $\lambda_-(4) = \beta'(2) \log 2 = \psi^3 \log 2 \approx -0.1636$, so $\lambda_-(4) - 2\lambda_-(2) = (\psi^3 - 2\psi^2) \log 2 = -\log 2$. The next theorem bounds the discrepancy.

::: {.theorem #thm-almost-additive title="Almost additivity of $\lambda_-$" lean="D5/S1/Deficit/AlmostAdditivity.lambdaMinus_almost_additive"}
For all natural numbers $m, n$ with $m \ge 1$ and $n \ge 1$,

$$
\bigl| \lambda_-(mn) - \lambda_-(m) - \lambda_-(n) \bigr| \le \log \operatorname{rad}(\gcd(m, n)).
$$
:::

In particular, when $m$ and $n$ are coprime the right-hand side is $\log 1 = 0$, so $\lambda_-(mn) = \lambda_-(m) + \lambda_-(n)$.

::: proof
Fix a prime $p$ and write $a = v_p(m)$, $b = v_p(n)$, so that $v_p(mn) = a + b$. A prime dividing only one of $m$ and $n$ contributes nothing, because $\beta'(0) = 0$; a common prime $p$ contributes $\bigl(\beta'(a+b) - \beta'(a) - \beta'(b)\bigr) \log p$. By [@thm-deficit-integer], the deficit equals its contraction-face reading $c(a, b) = \beta'(a) + \beta'(b) - \beta'(a+b)$, so

$$
\lambda_-(mn) - \lambda_-(m) - \lambda_-(n) = -\sum_{p \mid \gcd(m, n)} c\bigl(v_p(m), v_p(n)\bigr) \log p .
$$

By [@thm-deficit-three-valued] each term has $|c| \le 1$; by the triangle inequality, the absolute value of the left-hand side is at most $\sum_{p \mid \gcd(m, n)} \log p = \log \operatorname{rad}(\gcd(m, n))$. The Lean proof takes the same steps, and the decisive one is [`deficit_three_valued`](lean:D5/S1/Deficit/DeficitThreeValued.deficit_three_valued). The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The table lists a few cases computed from the identity above, with the values of $c$ taken from [@ex-small-deficits] in Chapter 1; the error column was also computed directly from the definition of $\lambda_-$. For all pairs with $1 \le m, n < 300$ the direct computation agrees with [@thm-almost-additive], and the error is $0$ whenever $m$ and $n$ are coprime. This is a finite check, not a proof.

| 2 | 2 | $2$: $(1, 1)$, $+1$ | $-\log 2 \approx -0.693$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |
| 4 | 4 | $2$: $(2, 2)$, $-1$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |
| 2 | 4 | $2$: $(1, 2)$, $0$ | $0$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |
| 6 | 6 | $2$: $(1, 1)$, $+1$; $3$: $(1, 1)$, $+1$ | $-\log 6 \approx -1.792$ | $\log 6 \approx 1.792$ |

$\lambda_-$ places the contraction-face reading on the exponents of primes. Placing the expansion-face reading there instead, and writing the result as an Euler product, leads to a question that is still open in trureturing. For a natural number $v$ let $S(v) = \lfloor (v+1)\varphi \rfloor - 1$ (the $s(v)$ of [@thm-beatty-deficit]), let $\psi = 1 - \varphi$, and

$$
\beta(v) = S(v) - v\psi .
$$

By the two closed forms in the proof of [@thm-beatty-deficit], this is exactly the expansion-face reading $\beta(v)$ of Chapter 1 (we also checked it value by value for $v < 500$); for example $\beta(1) = \varphi^2$ and $\beta(2) = \varphi^3$. For a complex number $s$, define the Euler germ

$$
E(s) = \prod_{p} \sum_{v=0}^{\infty} p^{-s\beta(v)}, \qquad p^{w} = e^{w \log p},
$$

the product running over all primes $p$.

::: {.conjecture #conj-independence title="The independence conjecture O-5"}
There is a function $Z \colon \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ such that:

1. $Z$ is meromorphic on the right half-plane $\{s : \operatorname{Re} s > 0\}$;
2. $Z(s) = E(s)$ for every $s$ with $\operatorname{Re} s > 1/\varphi^2$;
3. for every $s$ with $1/(2\varphi^3) < \operatorname{Re} s < 1/\varphi^2$: if $Z$ is analytic at $s$ and $Z(s) = 0$, then $\operatorname{Re} s = 1/(2\varphi^2)$.
:::

The three constants are $1/\varphi^2 \approx 0.382$, $1/(2\varphi^2) \approx 0.191$ and $1/(2\varphi^3) \approx 0.118$. Meromorphic and analytic are meant in Mathlib's sense[^conv].

Put $u = \varphi^2 s$. Since $\beta(1) = \varphi^2$, the $v = 1$ term of $E(s)$ is $p^{-\varphi^2 s} = p^{-u}$; the band in condition 3 becomes $1/(2\varphi) < \operatorname{Re} u < 1$, and the line becomes $\operatorname{Re} u = 1/2$ (the trureturing Blueprint page GoldenSpectralCoordinate records this change of scale). In this coordinate the line sits where the critical line of the Riemann $\zeta$ function does; this is only a formal analogy, and the trureturing README states plainly that the repository does not establish the Riemann hypothesis. $E$ is also related to $\lambda_-$: expanding the product formally (ignoring convergence), each positive integer $n$ contributes exactly one term $\prod_{p \mid n} p^{-s\beta(v_p(n))}$; by [@lem-two-faces], $\beta(v) = \beta'(v) + \sqrt5\, v$, so this term equals $n^{-\sqrt5 s} e^{-s\lambda_-(n)}$, and formally

$$
E(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-\sqrt5 s} e^{-s\lambda_-(n)} .
$$

This is only a formal manipulation; the book does not discuss where it holds.

O-5 is an open problem, not a theorem, and this book cites no Lean declaration for it. trureturing records it as `o5_independence` in `Hearts.lean`, whose proof is `sorry`; the module comment says the declaration records the open claim without asserting that it has been proved. The trureturing README and VISION documents both list O-5 as still having open obligations.

trureturing proves two results about O-5 in the module `GoldenWindowZeroObstruction`, and neither settles it. One says: if some function is analytic on a half-plane $\operatorname{Re} s > r$ (with $0 < r < 1/\varphi^2$), equals $E$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1/\varphi^2$, and has a zero inside that half-plane and inside the band of condition 3 whose real part differs from $1/(2\varphi^2)$, then no candidate that satisfies conditions 1 and 2 and, on $\operatorname{Re} s > r$, also a normal-form condition (Mathlib's `MeromorphicNFOn`) satisfies condition 3. The other says: changing the value of a meromorphic candidate at a single point makes it non-analytic there, which evades condition 3 at that point. The Blueprint concludes that the first does not refute the original O-5 statement, and that the second turns a limitation of that statement into a theorem.

What this section says about O-5 comes from these files at the pinned trureturing commit:

- `D5/X_Frontier/Hearts.lean`: the statement `o5_independence` and the definitions it uses;
- `README.md` and `docs/VISION.md`: O-5 listed as open;
- `Blueprint/D5/X_Frontier/GoldenWindowZeroObstruction.md`: the two proved results above;
- `Blueprint/D5/S1/Deficit/Beatty/GoldenSpectralCoordinate.md`: the change of scale $\varphi^2 \cdot 1/(2\varphi^2) = 1/2$.

[^conv]: In `Hearts.lean`, meromorphy of $Z$ is Mathlib's `MeromorphicOn`, which places no constraint on the values a function takes at its poles; condition 3 asks for $Z$ to be analytic at $s$ (`AnalyticAt`) precisely so that the zero test does not depend on those values. The infinite sum and product in $E$ are Lean's unconditional sum and product, which take conventional values when they do not converge (sum $0$, product $1$); the conjecture uses $E$ only where $\operatorname{Re} s > 1/\varphi^2$.
